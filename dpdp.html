Where it starts

It starts with a change in mindset

The old habit of collecting freely and keeping everything is now a legal liability. The shift has to happen across leadership, programme staff, field teams, and partners.

The old mindset

Collect freely, keep everything

  • Collect whatever we can.
  • Hold on to it in case we need it later.
  • More data is a good thing.
The new mindset

Collect with reason, delete by default

  • Only collect what we can justify.
  • Ask why before collecting anything.
  • Delete by default; keep only with a reason.
  • Access by role; log who used data, when, and why.
  • Data is a responsibility, not an asset.
How we work

Five stages to adoption

Getting compliant isn't a one-off task. It's a change the whole organisation goes through, and we work through it in five stages.

Stage 01

Change the mindset

Leadership briefings and staff sessions that make data protection everyone's job across the whole organisation, not just the IT team.

Stage 02

Set up a Data Protection team

A small independent team, usually two to five people, that reviews every data collection and can overrule programme teams. Nothing gets collected without its sign-off.

Stage 03

Two adoption pathways

New data follows DPDP rules from the start: notice, recorded consent, minimum collection. Old data starts from "delete", and survives only with a specific, documented reason.

Stage 04

Prepare for adoption

India data residency checks on every tool, consent-manager arrangements, data policies, and special-category safeguards for children and persons with disabilities.

Stage 05

Audit & certify

An outside independent agency reviews your policies, data, and systems, and certifies that you're following the Act.

The hard part

What to do with years of old data

Start by deleting it. "We might need it later" doesn't count. Where there's a genuine reason to keep something, pick the option that exposes the least data:

Preferred

Analyse, then delete

Pull out what you need as dashboards, tables, or written reports, then delete the underlying records. AI makes this quick.

If needed

Anonymise

Strip anything that identifies a person, but keep the dataset usable for analysis and longitudinal work.

Last resort

Encrypt

For cases where you need verifiable person-level records, such as a government or donor audit. Kept encrypted, access-logged, and locked down.

The non-negotiable: Indian beneficiary data stays inside India. The DP team checks where every tool stores its data, whether it is global SaaS, self-hosted, or third-party. If the vendor has an India region, get it in writing; if not, switch tools.

A special category

Extra care for children and persons with disabilities

Much of the sector works directly with children or persons with disabilities. The law sets a higher bar, and the DP team checks all four before any such data is collected:

  • Verifiable parental or guardian consent, in a way you can prove
  • Confirmed relationship between consent-giver and child
  • No tracking, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising
  • No use of data that could harm their well-being
The engagement

Four phases, six to twelve months

Phase 1

Set up

Form the independent DP team and adopt the principles organisation-wide.

Phase 2

Clean up

Sort old data (delete, analyse, anonymise, or encrypt) and move everything to India-based storage.

Phase 3

Get prepared

Consent-manager arrangements, data policies, and new-data MIS reviews.

Phase 4

Certify

An independent agency audits and certifies compliance.

At the end you hold an independent certificate to show your board and your donors.
